Repurchase Agreement Market

As part of this fixed-income and banking series, it would be almost impossible not to cover the repurchase agreement market. This is because repos are the engine of the whole financial system. But first, what actually is a repurchase agreement (repo)?

Mechanics of Repos:

Definition

Repos provide short-term cash loans and are tied to securities such as government bonds.

This allows banks, hedge funds, and other financial institutions to quickly get access to capital to deploy rapidly. An example is a hedge fund needing $100 million of leverage to amplify the profits they make on a trade. However, the opportunity window is only 1 hour before it goes, and the strategy can no longer be executed. So the hedge fund goes to a cash lender, who then makes a deal with them. The cash lender, normally a large institution with lots of capital reserves, such as money market mutual funds, the Reserve Bank of India, and corporate treasuries. The cash lender states that if the hedge fund hands over $100 million worth of treasury/G-sec bonds to hold overnight, they will provide $100 million of cash for the hedge fund to use quickly. The purpose of the risk-free bonds is to provide collateral so that if the hedge fund defaults, the lender has the right to sell the bonds to recoup the loan’s face value. So the next day, the cash lender charges you what’s known as the repo rate, which is the interest you have to pay for the cash provided. So what the hedge fund does is pay the repo rate, but they invest all the capital given to them, so how can they repay the full $100 million? The hedge fund uses what is known as a roll-over. They cancel the contract from the previous day and create a new one, which is an extension of the old one. The hedge fund buys time to hold the trade for longer. If they still do not want to close the trade, they just roll it over for another day and pay the repo rate again, and this process continues until they cash out of the position and repay the loan with repo-rate interest.

Thus, the reason why repos are associated with plumbing is that they are the waterworks that constantly move billions of dollars in the financial system.

The haircut:

The haircut in the repo market is a form of protection for the cash lender from market volatility. If the lender were to take on $100 million worth of bonds from the borrower in exchange for the $100 million they provided, and the borrower defaults and the price of the bonds crashes, then the lender would end up with a net loss. Thus, for the inventory risk the lender is taking on, they demand that the bond value cover the cash being provided plus an additional amount known as the haircut. So, for a $100 million loan, a repo purchaser may need $102 million (typical for Treasury bonds) worth of bonds to compensate for the risk for the cash lender. The size of the haircut is proportional to the riskiness of the loans being provided. Thus, junk bonds (bonds that have high interest but pose a severe threat of default) will require a much higher haircut.

Cash Provided $100,000,000
Haircut Percentage 2.0%
Required Collateral Value $102,000,000

If banks and hedge funds do not properly negotiate haircuts, or lenders are generally charging higher haircuts, then the bank or hedge fund’s operations will be fundamentally changed. Since banks constantly rely on repos to cover deficits and for many other purposes, if haircuts are too large (seen during economic crises, which will be covered later), then the bank will not be able to access repos at the same scale, due to each repo contract eating into the bank’s bond reserves. For hedge funds, fixed income desks in hedge funds can profit from tiny pricing discrepancies and arbitrage opportunities that yield tiny returns in comparison to other strategies. So to amplify these returns, funds turn to debt, which is often in the form of repos. Thus, if a hedge fund cannot get access to repos at the same speed, greater amounts of reserves are tied up at a given time.

How can high haircuts affect the whole economy?

In a previous article, we looked at the liquidity trap. This was because in recessions, people hoard money in safe-haven assets such as treasury or G-Sec bonds. Well, the same logic can be applied when haircuts rise. Haircuts rise usually due to a security that was seen as safe bursts, and lenders become more risk-averse when dealing with this asset (think of MBS in 2008). Or haircuts rise due to the whole economy seeing more collapses and defaults, so lenders require more collateral to shield them. Thus, to secure sufficient collateral for repos, financial institutions must scramble for risk-free bonds, which further sinks the economy into a liquidity trap and a near-zero interest rate environment since the velocity of money decreases.

Margin Calls:

Daily calculations known as mark-to-market calculations find the Required Collateral Value.

Required Collateral = Cash Loan × (1 + Repo Interest)1 − Haircut

If the value of the collateral held by the lender drops below the Required Collateral Value threshold, the lender can issue a margin call. This entails the lender requesting more collateral or cash to restore the Required Collateral Value. Due to sudden market shocks, this can sometimes lead financial institutions to panic-sell assets to free up cash to fulfill the margin call. This can have a spiral effect on the broader economy, as mass selling drives prices down.

Main types of Repos:

  • Overnight repos: Mature after one business day only and then must be rolled over or paid off
  • Term Repos: Mature on a pre-agreed date longer than overnight repos.
  • Bilateral Repos: A repo contract between 2 parties who take on the risk themselves and exchange everything themselves
  • Tri-party Repos: A repo contract that has a third party overseeing it to ensure regulations are met, manage collateral, and mark-to-market calculations.

Case Studies:

The first case study is the 2008 financial crisis and specifically the collapse of Lehman Brothers. Repo 105. An accounting trick used to give the illusion of financial health in a period of distress. The loophole:

Lehman Brothers: The Repo 105 Trick

1. THE LOOPHOLE

You take on a repo at over a 5% haircut, so for $100 million of cash, you would need to provide $105 million worth of secure assets. Thus, Lehman Brothers was able to use a loophole to sign this off as a sale. This was because they turned to British law; the regulations stated that, since they were giving a huge premium (haircut), it was a sale, since the lender had total control of the assets. Hence the absurdity and illogical nature of this accounting trick.

2. THE ILLUSION

Since it was classed as a sale, they could legally take the loan taken out off the balance sheet and, to further improve balance sheet and solvency ratios, pay off current liabilities using the cash from the repos.

3. THE REVERSAL

This would all occur just before the financial reports were to be released, so as soon as they were released, Lehman Brothers would repurchase the bonds.

The Absurdity of the Accounting Loophole

This indirectly led to the bankruptcy of a firm with around $600 billion in assets. Whilst their poor subprime mortgages were the main driver, the repo 105 caused trust to be lost in the financial sector. This meant that, in addition to haircuts already being high due to the collapse of MBS, Lehman Brothers experienced even higher haircuts due to this trust barrier. Eventually, their inability to meet current liabilities meant they had to file for bankruptcy.

The second case study is the cash-draining coincidence in 2019 that almost pulled the world into another economic meltdown.

The 2019 Repo Crisis

1. THE DRAIN

On the same day in September 2019, billions of dollars were paid in corporate tax, while simultaneously billions of dollars in newly issued Treasury bonds settled. The tax payments drained cash out of the banking system, while the bond settlements flooded the market with new risk-free securities.

2. THE SQUEEZE

This meant that banks and repo market participants were holding a massive surplus of bonds with virtually no cash available to fund them. Due to basic supply and demand, overnight repo rates violently spiked as cash became nearly impossible to obtain.

3. THE RESCUE

To prevent a total freeze of short-term funding markets, the Federal Reserve intervened, injecting billions in cash through repo operations so institutions could meet their daily liabilities. Had the Fed not acted as the lender of last resort, the plumbing failure could have triggered another systemic financial crisis.

When Wall Street's Plumbing Ran Dry

Whilst the 2019 federal intervention was a classic example of how the federal bank can inject cash back into the system, the Federal Reserve can perform what is known as a reverse repo to suck money out of the system and control inflation.

The Execution:

The Federal Reserve sells bonds as collateral for cash lenders to supply them with a short-term loan. This takes cash out, and whilst they repay the lender eventually, they can delay this either through roll-overs or by using a term reverse repo. In addition to this, the bank will then set the repo rate higher. By doing this, they drive up the repo rates, which controls the velocity of money and decreases inflation. The Federal Reserve drives up interest rates since the Federal Reserve is fundamentally a safer borrower than a commercial bank, so lenders charge commercial banks even higher repo rates.
